\abstract{%
  The rapid growth of low-Earth-orbit satellite constellations increasingly
  contaminates wide-field survey images with linear artefacts. Automated
  satellite-trail detection and masking are becoming a
  requirement for observatories.
  %
  This dissertation replicates the U-Net plus probabilistic Hough transform
  pipeline of \citet{stoppa2024} on a labelled MeerLICHT subset. The
  implementation uses an architecture-faithful U-Net, a combined binary
  cross-entropy and squared-Dice loss, validation-only model and threshold
  selection, and probabilistic Hough post-processing. It is examined through
  four areas: error analysis, model variants,
  training sensitivity, and inference-time strategies.
  %
  The replication reproduces the paper's recall, with test recall
  $0.923$ against $0.94$. Hough post-processing reproduces the paper's
  completeness effect: trail-pixel recall rises from $0.918$ to $0.990$, and the
  pixel-level false-negative rate falls from $0.082$ to $0.0105$, comparable to
  the paper's $6.98\,\%\rightarrow3.38\,\%$. Pixel precision remains below
  the paper and is prevalence-dependent.
  %
  The error analysis localises much of the gap near annotated trails:
  $82.5\,\%$ of false positives fall in trail-bearing patches; tolerance and
  distance checks place many within one pixel of the mask. A single-author
  re-annotation of $64$ crops does not
  support systematic width bias between the original masks, the re-annotation,
  and the model. Model, training, and inference variants do not close
  the precision gap either.
  Altogether, the evidence supports a narrow interpretation:
  boundary-scale annotation disagreement is the best-supported explanation for
  the precision gap on this dataset. The practical
  implication is that high-recall masking can still be useful when
  pixel precision is boundary-limited, and that thin-artefact detectors should
  report pixel scores alongside boundary-tolerant checks and mask-width
  conventions.
}

\chapter{Introduction and Context}
\label{ch:introduction}

The low-Earth-orbit satellite population is growing quickly. CelesTrak listed
15{,}848 active satellites on 27 May 2026, of which approximately 14{,}600
occupied orbits below 2{,}000\,km, including more than 10{,}000 Starlink
spacecraft \citep{celestrak2026}. Regulatory approvals extend further:
15{,}000 second-generation Starlink satellites are approved \citep{fcc2026}, and
policy reviews treat proposals exceeding 100{,}000 spacecraft as realistic
\citep{walker2020,walker2021}. Each sunlit satellite crossing a wide-field
exposure leaves a bright, near-linear trail that corrupts photometry
along its path and produces false-positive detections into
transient-detection pipelines \citep{tyson2020,hainaut2020}.

This contamination is already measurable. Starlink trails affected under
$0.5\,\%$ of Zwicky Transient Facility twilight images in late 2019 but
$18\,\%$ by August 2021 \citep{mroz2022}. Rubin Observatory simulations
indicate that approximately $10\,\%$ of LSST images would contain at least one
trail under a 40{,}000-satellite scenario \citep{rubin2026}. Other wide-field
twilight exposures can reach $30$--$40\,\%$ contamination
\citep{hainaut2020,bassa2022}, and $4.3\pm0.4\,\%$ of Hubble ACS/WFC images
taken in 2018--2021 contained trails \citep{kruk2023}. At these
rates manual flagging does not scale: a survey instrument such as MeerLICHT
\citep{bloemen2016} produces $10{,}560\times10{,}560$-pixel frames,
so identifying and masking affected pixels must be automated,
reliable, and cheap enough to run along the reduction pipeline.

\section{The target pipeline}
\label{sec:target_pipeline}

\citet{stoppa2024} present ASTA, the pipeline replicated in this
dissertation: a U-Net \citep{ronneberger2015} trained on manually annotated
MeerLICHT images, followed by a probabilistic Hough transform
\citep{hough1962,matas2000} applied to the network's output probability map.
The two stages serve complementary roles. The U-Net supplies per-pixel soft
detection that survives the crowded stellar background. The Hough stage then
adds an explicit global-line constraint, connecting fragmented detections and
extending partially recovered trails when the probability map already contains
weak linear evidence. The pipeline reported pixel precision and
recall of $0.94$ and was applied to around 200{,}000 full MeerLICHT fields.

\section{Background and related work}
\label{sec:related}


\subsection{Classical trail detection}
\label{sec:related_classical}

Classical satellite-trail detection treats trails as geometric line
features. Hough and Radon-family methods map image pixels into line-parameter
space, making them natural tools for long, straight artefacts
\citep{hough1962,duda1972,matas2000}. Astronomical uses include survey
cleaning, masked-star validation in crowded fields, faint-streak
filtering, and Hubble ACS trail recovery
\citep{storkey2004,bektesevic2017,nir2018,stark2026}.
A common limitation is sensitivity to faint, fragmented, or
background-confused trails, where reliable voting and instrument-specific
preprocessing become difficult; this motivates learned segmentation before
geometric post-processing.

\subsection{Deep learning for astronomical segmentation}
\label{sec:related_dl}

The U-Net architecture \citep{ronneberger2015} is an encoder--decoder with skip
connections that help recover fine spatial detail. It was designed for
biomedical segmentation and is now common in astronomical imaging. Close
precedents for thin, sparse artefacts are deepCR \citep{zhang2020} and Cosmic-CoNN
\citep{xu2023}, both cosmic-ray segmentation systems. MaxiMask
\citep{paillassa2020} covers contaminant masking including satellite and
aircraft trails, and trails have since been targeted with U-Net plus
line-segment detection \citep{chen2025}, multi-band attention U-Nets
\citep{yu2025}, and end-to-end streak cataloguing \citep{carrillo2026}. No
published trail-detection pipeline was found that gates a segmentation U-Net
with a lightweight patch classifier. Section~\ref{sec:ext_twostage} explores this.

\subsection{The target pipeline: Stoppa et al.\ (2024)}
\label{sec:related_target}

\citet{stoppa2024} trained a U-Net on $528\times528$ MeerLICHT patches with a
combined binary cross-entropy and Dice loss, and applied a probabilistic
Hough transform to the U-Net probability map as a post-processing step. On a
20{,}000-patch test set at threshold $0.58$ the pipeline reported
pixel precision and recall both approaching $0.94$, with the Hough step reducing
the pixel-level false-negative rate from $6.98\,\%$ to $3.38\,\%$. The paper does not report the
hyperparameters, making replication a non-trivial search (Chapter~\ref{ch:pipeline}).

\section{Aims and scope}
\label{sec:aims}

This dissertation has three aims. The first is a replication
of the \citet{stoppa2024} pipeline on the labelled MeerLICHT subset, with the
network architecture reconstructed from the released Keras \texttt{model-best.h5}
file. The second is to diagnose the error of that replication, asking how much of the
precision gap can be explained by evaluation population, boundary sensitivity,
and false positives. The third is to test which interventions can
move that gap, through model/training variants or inference-time strategies.

\chapter{Dataset Characteristics}

\section{The MeerLICHT subset}
\label{sec:meerlicht}

MeerLICHT is a $65$\,cm wide-field optical telescope \citep{bloemen2016}.
Its $10{,}560\times10{,}560$-pixel CCD samples a
$2.7$\,deg$^2$ field of view at $0.56''$\,pixel$^{-1}$, so a $528\times528$ patch
subtends about $4.9'$ on a side. The labelled subset comprises 178 full-frame
image--mask pairs: 8-bit PNG renders derived from FITS exposures, each paired
with a hand-annotated binary trail mask (Figure~\ref{fig:data_examples}). The
original FITS files and their headers were unavailable, so the dataset does
not include {WCS}, exposure, photometric-calibration, or observing-geometry
metadata. Therefore, analysis of sky-coordinate projection, horizon or
zenith angle, exposure-time-normalised trends, or calibrated flux behaviour is not attempted.

\section{Patch extraction and class imbalance}
\label{sec:patching}

Full frames are too large for direct training. Patches are extracted at
$528\times528$ pixels with stride $528$, yielding exactly $400$ non-overlapping
patches per image ($20\times20$ grid). Many trails cross adjacent patches, and
the natural patch distribution is highly imbalanced, with approximately
$93$--$94\,\%$ of full-grid patches containing no trail pixels. To form the
sampled patch manifests (patch lists used by the
training and evaluation), all positive patches are retained and negatives
are down-sampled to a $1{:}3$ positive-to-negative patch ratio. Figure~\ref{fig:data_flow}
gives the realised 528-pixel manifest sizes used by the final run.

The sampled test set is the primary test population since it has the
same $1{:}3$ pos:neg prior as the validation set used for threshold
selection. A separate parity test set keeps every test-frame patch, including
all negatives, to restore the natural empty-patch prevalence for comparison with
the paper (Figure~\ref{fig:data_flow}). This separation is crucial because adding many empty
patches primarily changes precision: recall is computed over retained positive
patches, whereas the additional negatives can introduce false positives.

\section{Dataset splits}
\label{sec:splits}

Figure~\ref{fig:data_flow} summarises the path from frames to evaluation
manifests. Splits are performed at image level, never after patching, so
every patch from a given frame remains in the same split. The 178
images have skewed trail-pixel counts and are stratified by total trail-pixel
count into four quartile groups. The nominal $0.70/0.15/0.15$ ratios are
applied within each group with flooring; the remainder goes to test. The realised split is
\textbf{122 train / 24 validation / 32 test} ($0.685/0.135/0.180$).
Because splitting happens before patch extraction, this prevents
patches from the same source image appearing in different splits and reserves a
relatively large held-out fraction for evaluation.

\section{Mask quality}
\label{sec:mask_quality}

Beyond visual inspection, connected-component measurements provide a simple
mask-quality check. Components have high aspect ratios and a median stroke width
near $6$\,px, estimated from distances to the nearest mask edge, matching
long, thin annotated structures. The densest-patch overlays show masks following
the visible trails, while exposing occasional endpoint under-coverage
(Figure~\ref{fig:mask_quality}).

The masks are sufficient for supervised training, with characterised caveats.
Very small isolated labels, where present, are retained because the reproduction
target is the supplied masks. Full-frame overlays also show occasional
under-coverage at faint endpoints and low-contrast stretches. If the model
detects a visible trail segment omitted from the mask, those pixels are still
counted as false positives. Thus, label-referenced precision is conservative
where masks omit visible trail pixels. Section~\ref{sec:ext_boundary} explores
this sensitivity.

\chapter{Methodological Framing}
\label{ch:methodology}

\section{Problem formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

Trail detection is posed as patch-wise binary pixel segmentation. Given a
$528\times528$ single-channel patch, the model estimates a
per-pixel probability that each pixel belongs to a satellite trail rather than
background. Thus, the required output is a mask for downstream cleaning, not
a catalogue of satellite identities or trail instances.

This is semantic segmentation: pixels are labelled by class, not assigned to
individual trail instances. Crossing or near-parallel trails within a patch are
simply all ``trail'', so pixel-level metrics
cannot distinguish merged from separated trails. The formulation is label-referenced:
success is measured against the available binary masks, so boundary placement
and mask under-coverage impact precision.

\section{Segmentation with Hough post-processing}
\label{sec:why_method}

The pipeline pairs a learned segmentation stage with a classical geometric stage.
A U-Net produces a soft-detection map that preserves weak, spatially extended
evidence of a faint trail. A classical Hough detector applied directly to the
raw image responds mainly to stars, galaxies, and diffraction
structure. In a validation-tuned raw-image Hough implementation, precision was
only about $10^{-3}$, showing that this Hough voting is an insufficient
masking baseline. Thus, the probabilistic Hough transform is used after the
U-Net, where the input is already a trail-probability canvas rather than a
crowded sky image. It can exploit the prior that a trail is globally
linear to bridge gaps, recover continuous trail structure, and support
high-recall masking. The methodological choices follow this: the U-Net
supplies pixel-level segmentation, the BCE--Dice loss handles severe foreground
imbalance, and evaluations separate selection from label-boundary effects.

\section{U-Net architecture choice}
\label{sec:why_unet}

The U-Net encoder--decoder with skip connections \citep{ronneberger2015}
preserves fine spatial detail by combining matching decoder and
encoder features. This is important for thin, elongated, sparse targets, while
its encoder path still supplies enough context to distinguish trails from
stellar structure and background gradients. The same architecture underpins
astronomical artefact detectors such as deepCR \citep{zhang2020} and MaxiMask
\citep{paillassa2020}, supporting suitability here.

More elaborate variants are explored in Section~\ref{sec:ext_attention}, which
tests whether adding attention changes the error pattern. Transformer-style
segmenters such as ViT are not explored since they
would change the detector family and need far more tuning and data to
evaluate fairly on this small labelled subset.

\section{Combined BCE and Dice loss}
\label{sec:why_loss}

The foreground occupies a tiny fraction of pixels, so a pure cross-entropy
objective is dominated by empty background. The Dice term measures region overlap
and is less dominated by the background majority \citep{milletari2016}.
Combining it with binary cross-entropy retains a per-pixel classification term
while following the Combo-loss pattern for severely imbalanced segmentation
\citep{taghanaki2019}. The final pipeline uses an unweighted BCE term; class
imbalance is handled through $1{:}3$ patch sampling and the Dice component,
since additional positive weighting would favour over-prediction.

\section{Evaluation and assumptions}
\label{sec:eval_philosophy}

Model and threshold selection are fixed in advance and validation-only,
so no test metric is inspected before the configuration is fixed.
Sampled and parity test metrics are kept distinct: the
sampled set matches the validation prior, while the parity set restores the
natural empty-patch prevalence used for paper comparison. Uncertainty
treats the source image as the independent unit since patches
from one frame share background, atmospheric image quality, and annotation
structure. Finally, because masks omit some visible trail pixels (Section~\ref{sec:mask_quality}),
pixel-overlap scores are complemented by topology-aware (clDice),
false-positive distance, and boundary-tolerant metrics (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}).

\chapter{Model Implementation and Training}
\label{ch:pipeline}

\section{Data pipeline and augmentation}
\label{sec:datapipeline}

The patch builder implements the extraction scheme from
Section~\ref{sec:patching} by writing $528\times528$ stride-$528$ patches at
the $1{:}3$ sampling ratio. Each manifest row records the source image,
split, patch coordinates, positive-pixel fraction, and full-image mean and standard
deviation. The last two fields implement the \texttt{full\_image}
normalisation mode: each patch is converted to $[0,1]$ and normalised with its
parent image statistics, matching the paper's per-image standardisation
rather than normalising each patch.

\label{sec:augmentation}

Training patches are augmented with random horizontal flips,
vertical flips, and $k\times90^{\circ}$ rotations applied identically to each
image and mask (validation and test patches are not augmented). Furthermore, training
images receive signal-dependent stochastic noise (masks and evaluation splits do not).
This is not modelled as Poisson--Gaussian CCD noise since the inputs are display renders
rather than calibrated electron counts.
For a patch-pixel value $p\in[0,1]$ the augmentation samples
\[
  p_{\mathrm{noisy}} =
  \mathrm{clip}\!\left(p + \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\;
  m\sqrt{\alpha p + \beta^{2}}\right),\,0,\,1\right),
\]
where $m$ is the noise multiplier and $\alpha,\beta$ are calibrated once from
empty-mask training patches, so the injected noise matches the measured background
variation. The chosen calibration uses $9{,}894$ empty train patches, giving
$\alpha=0.0209$ and $\beta=0.0886$. A preliminary three-seed screen compared a
no-noise control with multipliers $m\in\{0.5,1.0,2.0\}$, and the active configuration
fixes $m=1.0$ for robustness since validation-$F_1$ gain was not decisive.

\section{Network architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

The network shown in Figure~\ref{fig:unet} was reconstructed from the
released Stoppa et al. Keras \texttt{model-best.h5} configuration.
It is an encoder--decoder with skip connections,
filters progressing $8\to16\to32\to64\to128$, average-pooling downsampling,
transposed-convolution upsampling, and LeakyReLU activations with slope $0.3$.
Each double-convolution block follows
\texttt{Conv2d $\to$ LeakyReLU $\to$ Dropout $\to$ Conv2d $\to$ LeakyReLU}, with
element-wise dropout in all nine blocks. The dropout rates are [0.1,\ 0.1,\ 0.2,\ 0.2,\ 0.3,\ 0.2,\ 0.2,\ 0.1,\ 0.1].
The network uses no batch normalisation. The $3\times3$ convolutions use He-normal initialisation
\citep{he2015delving}; the transposed convolutions and the final $1\times1$ head use
Glorot-uniform initialisation with all biases initialised to zero. Inputs are
$528\times528$ single-channel patches and the output is a
single-channel logit map with identical spatial dimensions. Sigmoid conversion
is applied outside the model in the loss, thresholding, Hough, and visualisation paths. Altogether, the
network has $485{,}673$ trainable parameters (\texttt{base\_channels}~$=8$).

\section{Loss function}
\label{sec:loss}

Training uses a combined binary cross-entropy (BCE) and squared-Dice loss:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \lambda_{\mathrm{BCE}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}
              + \lambda_{\mathrm{Dice}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Dice}},
  \qquad \lambda_{\mathrm{Dice}} = 1 - \lambda_{\mathrm{BCE}},
\end{equation}
where
\[
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Dice}}
  = 1 - \frac{2\sum_i p_i g_i + \epsilon}
             {\sum_i p_i^{2} + \sum_i g_i^{2} + \epsilon},
  \qquad \epsilon = 10^{-4},
\]
with $p_i$ denoting the sigmoid probability and $g_i$ the binary target. The
BCE term uses PyTorch's BCE-with-logits loss without a class-balancing
\texttt{pos\_weight}, matching the paper and avoiding a second imbalance
correction on top of the sampled patch prior. Losses are evaluated in FP32 even though the
forward pass uses BF16 autocast, avoiding low-precision accumulation in the BCE
and Dice reductions.

\section{Hyperparameter search}
\label{sec:optuna}

Hyperparameter search uses Optuna \citep{akiba2019} with a tree-structured
Parzen estimator and median pruner ($n_{\mathrm{startup}}=15$, 10 warm-up
epochs). The 45 trials are equally balanced across batch sizes $\{8,16,32\}$.
Within each batch-size, the continuous hyperparameters are learning rate and BCE weight
$\lambda_{\mathrm{BCE}}$; dropout, normalisation, and noise multiplier are
fixed. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:hparam_space} gives the full search-space and
fixed-configuration table. Each trial runs for 20 epochs and is scored by
threshold-swept validation $F_1$, matching the final selection criterion.
Pruning uses in-epoch validation Dice at the fixed training threshold as an
early-pruning signal; only completed trials are rescored by swept
validation $F_1$. The top five trials by validation $F_1$ are retrained for 75
epochs at five seeds ($2804, 1234, 42, 7, 13$), with the winner chosen by mean
threshold-swept validation $F_1$ across seeds \emph{before any test metric is
inspected}. All stochastic training runs set Python, NumPy, PyTorch, and
DataLoader seeds; remaining training variability is reported through the
five-seed restudy. The fixed tie-breaker, applied when means fall within
$0.001$ validation $F_1$, prefers simpler/lower-risk configurations.
The selected base U-Net is trial~44 (\texttt{batch\_size}=32,
learning rate $2.89\times10^{-4}$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{BCE}}=0.655$), with
mean validation $F_1=0.854$ across the retrained seeds.

\section{Threshold selection}
\label{sec:threshold}

A precision--recall curve is swept on the validation set to select the
$F_1$-optimal threshold, which is applied unchanged to the test evaluation.
Each reported seed uses its own validation-selected threshold, with $0.45$ for
the primary seed 2804 (the paper fixes $0.58$). Within each run, the saved
best epoch is selected by validation Dice at the fixed
training threshold. That checkpoint is then swept over thresholds on the
validation set, and the resulting best validation $F_1$ is used for trial ranking
and selecting the probability threshold used at test time.

\section{Hough post-processing}
\label{sec:hough}

A probabilistic Hough transform \citep{matas2000} (\texttt{cv2.HoughLinesP}) is
applied to a lower-threshold probability canvas. It recovers faint, linear detections
below the segmentation threshold. Its role is completeness, with precision
assessed separately because of the line-drawing convention.
For each test image the per-patch sigmoid probability maps are max-pooled into
a canvas before Hough voting. The sampled evaluation uses the $1{:}3$ patch
support, while the parity Hough comparison uses full $528$\,px tiling over every
test image. A full-coverage tiled pass is used as an implementation check against
patch-sampling artefacts. The canvas is thresholded at $t_{\mathrm{Hough}}=0.1$,
after which \texttt{HoughLinesP} is run with a vote threshold of $50$, a minimum
line length of $100$\,px, and a maximum gap of $250$\,px, matching the implementation
from \citet{stoppa2024}. Detected segments are drawn back onto the canvas at $3$\,px
thickness, making line rendering a comparability caveat for precision.

\section{Patch CNN classifier gate}
\label{sec:classifier}

A two-stage variant tests whether a patch-level gate can reduce
empty-patch false positives and U-Net inference cost without losing trail-bearing
patches. Section~\ref{sec:ext_twostage} explores placing a lightweight
convolutional neural network patch classifier in front of the U-Net. The
classifier is simple: a four-block strided encoder followed by
global average pooling and a single fully connected logit head. It is trained on
the sampled training manifest with labels derived from the patch
positive-pixel fraction and a \texttt{pos\_weight} equal to the train-split
negative-to-positive ratio.
Validation reports both the maximum-$F_1$ and a high-recall
($\ge0.99$) operating point, because a patch rejected by the gate is
unrecoverable downstream. The classifier and U-Net are trained independently
and composed at inference, so the gate changes the pipeline without
retraining the segmentation model.

\section{Evaluation metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

Segmentation quality is reported with pixel precision, recall, Dice (equal to
$F_1$ for binary masks), and IoU. Since trails are thin connected structures,
pixel overlap alone can miss whether a prediction preserves centreline continuity.
Therefore, topology is assessed with the centreline Dice
(clDice) \citep{shit2021}. Sensitivity to boundary placement is assessed with a
boundary-tolerant precision/recall/$F_1$ score at
$k\in\{0,1,2,3\}$\,px, where $k=0$ is the strict metric and $k>0$ allows a
predicted pixel and a ground-truth pixel to match within a small spatial
tolerance. The spatial structure of false positives is summarised by their
distance to the nearest ground-truth trail pixel, with whole-patch false
positives reported separately as they have no defined trail distance.
Uncertainty is quantified by a cluster bootstrap over source images,
resampling images with replacement and aggregating their patches before
recomputing metrics. Patch-level bootstrap is reported as a secondary check.

\section{Replication of the target detector}
\label{sec:replication}

The validation-selected winner (trial~44; Section~\ref{sec:selection}) is
evaluated on the held-out test images at its selected threshold of $0.45$.

\section{Hough improves pixel completeness}
\label{sec:hough_results}

Probabilistic Hough post-processing raises trail-pixel recall from $0.918$ to
$0.990$ for the seed-2804 run on full-coverage parity canvas, equivalently
reducing the pixel-level false-negative rate from $0.082$ to $0.0105$
(five-seed mean $0.077\rightarrow0.0098$). This missed-pixel reduction is
comparable to the paper's $6.98\,\%\rightarrow3.38\,\%$. The full-coverage tiled
pass described in Section~\ref{sec:hough} reproduced the parity canvas
pixel-for-pixel, ruling out a patch-sampling or seam artefact. The image-level
detection false-negative rate is degenerate here: every positive test image is
already hit by at least one predicted pixel, so the rate is $0$ both before and
after Hough and is not comparable to the paper's image-level rate. At patch
level, the rate falls only from $0.122$ to $0.103$, since this stricter check
counts trail-bearing patches with no recovered pixel. Thus, Hough is treated as
a high-recall masking stage, with precision evaluated separately.

Figure~\ref{fig:hough_gap} shows Hough in action: the U-Net misses a trail
stretch over a bright star, and Hough recovers ground-truth pixels that
appear only after the geometric stage.

\section{Uncertainty quantification}
\label{sec:bootstrap}

Using a cluster bootstrap over the 32 held-out test images ($1{,}000$ resamples,
single-seed winner), the $95\,\%$ intervals are precision $[0.806,\,0.891]$,
recall $[0.888,\,0.946]$, Dice $[0.857,\,0.904]$, and IoU $[0.749,\,0.825]$.
These image-level intervals are several times wider than the patch-level
bootstrap, represented by Dice $[0.876,\,0.892]$. Patches from the same exposure
share background, trail structure, and annotation structure, so the image-cluster
interval is the headline interval. Across the five trial-44 seeds, variation is
smaller but still visible: precision $0.847\pm0.010$, recall $0.923\pm0.007$,
and Dice $0.884\pm0.004$.

%% Error localisation (the core finding)
\section{Precision gap near boundaries}
\label{sec:ext_error}

The analyses below separate where the false positives occur from why
they occur. Most false-positive mass is in trail-bearing patches, and the distance
analysis shows that a majority of false-positive pixels lie within one pixel of
labelled trails. This supports the boundary-scale interpretation, while
the smaller inter-patch component consists of predictions in labelled trail-free
patches which cannot be explained by mask-boundary placement.
Section~\ref{sec:ext_reannotation} tests this reading against a single-author re-annotation.

\subsection{False-positive decomposition}
\label{sec:ext_fpdecomp}

Decomposing the false-positive pixels of the winning model shows that only
$17.5\,\%$ are inter-patch (the network lighting up in trail-free patches), while
$82.5\,\%$ are intra-patch (falling inside trail-bearing patches, though not
necessarily adjacent to the trail) (Table~\ref{tab:fp_decomposition}). A patch-level
gate can only remove the inter-patch share, so $17.5\,\%$ is the maximum
false-positive mass it could remove. Hard-negative mining targets the same
failure mode during retraining (Section~\ref{sec:ext_dataeff}), so this value
is a rough scale for the possible effect.

  \centering
  \caption{False-positive pixel decomposition. Only inter-patch false positives
           are directly removable by a patch-level gate.}
  \label{tab:fp_decomposition}
  \begin{tabular}{lrr}
    \toprule
    Component & Pixels & Share \\
    \midrule
    Intra-patch FP & 300{,}745 & 82.5\% \\
    Inter-patch FP & 63{,}662 & 17.5\% \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Boundary-tolerant evaluation}
\label{sec:ext_boundary}

Re-scoring with symmetric boundary tolerances of $k\in\{0,1,2,3\}$\,px tests how
much of the score is sensitive to boundary placement rather than larger misses
(Figure~\ref{fig:boundary_tolerance}). A predicted pixel and a ground-truth pixel
are allowed to match if they fall within the chosen tolerance.
Allowing a single pixel of tolerance, the five-seed mean precision rises
from $0.847$ (strict) to $0.946$, recall from
$0.923$ to $0.980$, and the $\pm1$\,px $F_1$ reaches $0.962$. The tolerant score
is therefore diagnostic and should not be compared directly with the paper's pixel score.
It nevertheless shows that the precision gap to the paper largely closes under
a one-pixel tolerance. A one-sided check separates this from a
recall-for-precision trade: re-scoring the same predictions against ground
truth dilated by one pixel raises precision from $0.852$ to $0.937$ but drops
recall to $0.793$, whereas the symmetric tolerance raises both. This supports
the interpretation that much of the error is boundary-scale, with a smaller
component far from any labelled trail. The tolerant score therefore only
probes sensitivity: it relaxes the
pixel-matching rule and is not directly comparable to the paper's $0.94/0.94$.

\subsection{False-positive distance to mask}
\label{sec:ext_fpdist}

The distance from each false-positive pixel to the nearest ground-truth trail
pixel concentrates at small values (Figure~\ref{fig:fp_distance}). Across all
false positives, $57\,\%$ lie within $1$\,px of a labelled trail.
Restricting the denominator to distance-defined false positives, which excludes
the $\sim17\,\%$ whole-patch component, raises this share to $69\,\%$, with a
median distance of $1.6$\,px. Both denominators are reported because the $69\,\%$ value
excludes whole-patch false positives, so it would overstate the share of all
false positives that are boundary-near. Together, these denominators support a
boundary-near explanation for much of the
precision gap, while leaving a smaller genuine-background component that
boundary tolerance cannot explain.

\subsection{Relative display-contrast checks}
\label{sec:ext_faint}

The winner was rescored post hoc on the 31 positive test images using raw
8-bit PNG intensities as a \emph{relative display-contrast proxy}
(Appendix Figures~\ref{fig:faint_streak}--\ref{fig:fp_intensity}). This is not
calibrated flux or signal-to-noise, since the original count data are unavailable
(Section~\ref{sec:meerlicht}).

Across 55 cleaned trail components split into three approximately equal
relative-contrast bins, the lowest-contrast bin has the weakest pre-Hough recall
($0.853$) and the largest additional ground-truth recovery from Hough ($0.092$).
After Hough, recall is at least $0.945$ in all three bins.

Near-trail false positives have median display excess between the background and
labelled-trail medians (Figure~\ref{fig:fp_intensity}). This is compatible with
some boundary under-coverage, but remains descriptive rather than evidence of
label error.

\subsection{Topology-aware comparison (clDice)}
\label{sec:ext_cldice}

As a topology check, clDice explores whether the predictions preserve the trail
centreline structure, rather than only overlapping the labelled pixels. The
centreline Dice tracks pixel Dice and preserves the architecture ordering:
$0.847$ for the base model versus $0.838$ for the attention variant ($-0.009$),
against pixel Dice $0.884$ and $0.879$ ($-0.005$). Thus, clDice gives the
same null conclusion as pixel Dice: the attention model
does not recover more continuous trail structure on this dataset.

\subsection{Re-annotation audit}
\label{sec:ext_reannotation}

The prior analyses localise the error but leave two explanations:
visible trails may be wider than their masks, or the model may
systematically over-paint. To separate these, a single-author,
evaluation-only re-annotation was performed on $64$ sealed $528\times528$
crops. The set covered a range of display contrasts, including interior
and endpoint trail crops for boundary scoring, plus false-positive-prone and
quiet decoy crops as existence controls.

The first check is mask width. The original MeerLICHT masks, the re-annotation,
and the model prediction all have median stroke width $6$\,px. Per-crop width
differences are centred near zero: re-annotation minus original is $+0.10$\,px
($95\%$ CI $[-0.30,+0.53]$), and model minus re-annotation is $+0.22$\,px
($[-0.09,+0.54]$). These values do not support a systematic width bias, so the
false-positive gap cannot be explained simply by labels being uniformly too thin
or by the model being uniformly too wide.

The second check is pixel agreement. On the $44$ interior and endpoint crops,
the model matches the re-annotation ($F_1=0.893$) about as closely as the
original labels do ($0.890$), while the model and original labels score $0.894$
against each other (Table~\ref{tab:reannotation}, pixel-weighted micro-averages).
A paired per-crop bootstrap ($B=10{,}000$, equal weight per crop) gives a
model-minus-original difference against the re-annotation of $-0.0001$
($95\%$ CI $[-0.024,+0.022]$). At $\pm1$\,px the model is slightly lower
($\Delta=-0.015$, CI $[-0.038,+0.003]$), with the interval just spanning zero.
Because the model was trained on the original labels, this is directional support
rather than independent validation. The pattern is stable across groups,
with median widths of $6$\,px and $F_1$ near $0.86$ on interior segments and
$0.89$ on endpoints.

The control crops test whether the model marks signal where the re-annotation
does not. On the $11$ false-positive-prone controls, the model detects signal in
all cases, while the re-annotation marks a trail in only $5$. On the six
remaining crops, the median extra model area is $134$\,px, about $0.05\,\%$ of a
crop. The quiet decoy crops produce no model detections. Figure~\ref{fig:supp_gold_audit}
shows overlays and Figure~\ref{fig:supp_gold_audit_process} documents the
annotation process. Since this audit had only one annotator, it measures
disagreement between the original labels, the re-annotation, and the model. Its
value is in testing whether a large width mismatch is visible; a multi-annotator
audit remains the most valuable check (Section~\ref{sec:future}).

%% Model variants as tests of the error diagnosis
\section{Model variants as error checks}
\label{sec:ext_arch}

The model variants below are diagnostic checks, with the selected U-Net retained
as the baseline. If the precision gap were mainly caused by empty-patch false
positives, a patch classifier should improve precision. If it reflected missing
faint or continuous trail structure, attention gates might improve overlap or
topology. The results are limited: the gate gives a bounded precision shift in
line with the $17.5\,\%$ inter-patch false-positive share, and attention does not
improve the base architecture.

\subsection{Classifier-gated detector}
\label{sec:ext_twostage}

The classifier-gated detector uses the CNN described in Section~\ref{sec:classifier}.
Results are reported as a Pareto front over classifier and U-Net thresholds
(Figure~\ref{fig:two_stage_pareto}). Since the gate can only suppress
empty-patch false positives, its possible precision gain is bounded by the
inter-patch share.

At the illustrative high-recall operating point
($t_{\mathrm{clf}}=0.18$, $t_{\mathrm{U\mbox{-}Net}}=0.45$), the gate routes
$2{,}625$ patches to the U-Net ($75.3\,\%$ of the sampled test set). Precision
rises from $0.852$ to $0.864$, recall falls from $0.918$ to $0.914$, and Dice is
$0.888$. Classifier-rejected positive patches count as false negatives, so the
classifier false-negative rate ($0.096$) sets a recall ceiling.

The gate therefore gives a bounded precision shift and a real but modest compute
saving, skipping $24.7\,\%$ of sampled-test U-Net forwards. Larger savings would
be expected only on full-grid evaluation, where empty patches dominate.

  \caption{Two-stage operating-characteristic Pareto front over classifier and

\subsection{Attention-gated U-Net}
\label{sec:ext_attention}

The attention-gated U-Net \citep{oktay2018} is a near-capacity-matched variant,
carrying roughly $1\,\%$ more parameters than the base U-Net. It is trained under
the same search and validation-only selection protocol as the base model
(Section~\ref{sec:optuna}).

The selected attention model reaches test precision $0.838$, recall $0.924$, and
Dice $0.879$ at threshold $0.48$, against the base Dice of $0.884$. Across five
seeds, the test means differ by at most $0.005$
(Appendix Table~\ref{tab:base_vs_attention}). The validation-restudy means are
$0.854$ for the base model ($90\,\%$ interval $[0.851,\,0.856]$) and $0.851$ for
the attention model ($[0.850,\,0.852]$).

The result is therefore null under the matched protocol, not evidence for a
decisive architectural ranking. Together with the clDice result
(Section~\ref{sec:ext_cldice}), it gives no evidence that attention recovers
more continuous or faint trail structure on this dataset. Figure~\ref{fig:fp_fn_gallery_attn}
shows the attention-model gallery.

\subsection{Data-side sensitivity}
\label{sec:ext_dataeff}
\label{sec:ext_hardneg}

Two data-side checks ask whether more training images or targeted empty-patch
examples materially change the precision gap.

Retraining the winning configuration on nested $30/50/70/100\,\%$ subsets of the
training images, with validation and test sets fixed, gives an indicative
learning curve (Figure~\ref{fig:data_efficiency}). The curve is indicative
because hyperparameters are transferred from the $100\,\%$ winner and each
fraction is trained once. Validation Dice is flat to $50\,\%$, rises at
$70\,\%$, and is then near-constant. Most of the gain appears before the final
training fraction, suggesting that training-set size is unlikely to be the main
limiting factor for this architecture.

Hard negatives were mined by running the model on labelled-empty patches and
adding false-positive empty patches back into the training set. Adding 150 mined
hard negatives gives a small precision gain in a retrain: $+0.007$
($0.852\rightarrow0.859$), with essentially unchanged recall ($-0.001$) and
$+0.003$ Dice. The precision shift is within the five-seed dispersion
($\pm0.010$; Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap}), so the result is suggestive only.
Its scale is consistent with the $17.5\,\%$ inter-patch false-positive share
from Section~\ref{sec:ext_fpdecomp}.

\subsection{Soft labels and inference variants}
\label{sec:ext_softlabel}
\label{sec:ext_ensemble}
\label{sec:ext_probhough}

These checks test whether the precision gap can be reduced by changing the
training target or the inference procedure, while leaving the selected U-Net
architecture intact.

The dilated soft-label pilot softens the target near trail boundaries. The
trail core remains labelled $1$, while a $1$\,px dilated boundary band receives
an intermediate target value; validation and test masks remain hard-labelled.
The main effect is wider predictions, with no localisation gain. Recall rises by
$+0.008$, but precision falls by $-0.022$; exact Dice falls by $-0.009$,
$\pm1$\,px $F_1$ is essentially unchanged ($-0.001$), and clDice falls by
$-0.013$. Simply widening the training target therefore does not close the
boundary-sensitive residual.

Two inference-time variants give similarly small changes. A five-checkpoint
ensemble with test-time augmentation reaches Dice $0.8885$ (precision $0.852$,
recall $0.929$ at threshold $0.44$), compared with $0.8836$ for the selected
single model. The $+0.005$ Dice change is comparable to five-seed dispersion and
adds inference cost. Replacing the binary Hough input with a probability-stratified
canvas changes pixel recall by only $+0.00003$, leaves the patch-level
false-negative rate unchanged, and raises Hough false-positive pixels from
$2{,}725{,}702$ to $2{,}785{,}274$. The binary Hough stage therefore appears
recall-saturated on this dataset.

\section{Interpreting the headline comparison}
\label{sec:interpretation}

The differences in Section~\ref{sec:deviations} make recall the closest
like-for-like quantity. Even this comparison spans different populations: the
32 test images used here and the paper's 20{,}000 patches with unreported class
balance. For precision, the class-balance effect is measured directly. At
identical recall and fixed threshold, precision falls from $0.852$ to $0.780$.
Section~\ref{sec:ext_error} then places much of the remaining error near
annotated boundaries, supporting boundary-scale annotation disagreement as the
leading interpretation. Evaluating at the paper's fixed $0.58$ instead of the
swept $0.45$ raises precision by only $0.025$, so threshold choice is a minor
contributor.

The Hough stage improves pixel completeness (Section~\ref{sec:hough_results})
but lowers pixel precision from $0.780$ to $0.410$. A line-thickness sweep
attributes about half of this drop to the $3$\,px line-drawing convention
(Figure~\ref{fig:supp_hough_thickness}). On the full-coverage tiled canvas,
verified pixel-for-pixel against the parity canvas, the result is therefore
evidence of comparable completeness. The tolerant scores carry the same caution:
the $\pm1$\,px precision $0.946$ shows that much of the gap is sensitive to
one-pixel boundary placement, and it is kept separate from the paper's
$0.94/0.94$. The same pattern holds across the five seeds
(Figure~\ref{fig:supp_multiseed}).

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Six limitations define the scope of these findings.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mask under-coverage.} The annotations occasionally omit
        faint endpoints and low-contrast stretches
        (Section~\ref{sec:mask_quality}). The effect is asymmetric: a correct
        detection of an unlabelled trail pixel scores as a false positive,
        and a model trained on such labels may also learn to suppress faint
        extensions. Label-referenced precision can therefore understate detector
        performance where masks omit visible trail pixels. The re-annotation
        (Section~\ref{sec:ext_reannotation}) finds no systematic width difference
        between the original labels and the re-annotation; a multi-annotator
        audit would better quantify any mask under-coverage.
  \item \textbf{Dataset size.} The subset holds 178 images, of which 32 form
        the test set. Image-level bootstrap intervals are therefore several
        times wider than patch-level ones (Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap}), the
        image-level detection rate has little room to separate models, and the
        protocol-variation checks each use a single seed.
  \item \textbf{Sampled evaluation distribution.} The sampled test set keeps
        a $1{:}3$ positive-to-negative patch prior, which inflates precision
        relative to the full-image patch population, where roughly $93\,\%$ of
        patches are empty. The
        parity evaluation restores the negatives while retaining the
        sampled-validation threshold. The sampled-to-parity precision drop
        ($0.852$ to $0.780$) measures the resulting shift; recall is
        unaffected because all positive patches are retained.
  \item \textbf{Hough line-drawing convention.} The parity Hough canvas was
        verified as a full-coverage tiled prediction, ruling out patch sampling
        as the source of this sensitivity. The remaining sensitivity comes from
        the $3$\,px line-drawing convention.
  \item \textbf{Single telescope.} All training and quantitative evaluation
        are confined to MeerLICHT, leaving transfer across instruments with
        different pixel scales, point-spread functions, or backgrounds
        untested. The DECam montage
        (Figure~\ref{fig:decam_cold_montage}) therefore provides a qualitative
        transfer check.
  \item \textbf{PNG-only inputs.} The 8-bit PNG renders cap the dynamic range
        and remove WCS, exposure, and photometric metadata. Contrast statements
        in Section~\ref{sec:ext_faint} are therefore measurements on the
        rendered PNG scale.
        The direction of any bias is unclear, because the labels were drawn
        on the same renders. The analysis is also single-channel, leaving any
        effect of multi-band imagery on trail separability or morphology
        untested \citep{yu2025}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Scientific implications}
\label{sec:implications}

Beyond the replication, the results matter for astronomical survey practice
because they separate detector utility from label-referenced scoring. A
detector may recover enough trail extent to support masking even when pixel
precision is reduced by boundary-level annotation choices. The analysis
therefore has three implications for reporting, evaluation, and practical trail
masking.

First, where masks omit visible trail pixels, label-referenced precision
can be conservative. The Hough stage illustrates this: it extrapolates linear
extent the network under-detects, and any recovered pixels outside the
annotation score as false positives. The metric can therefore penalise the
recovery the stage was built for. Reported precision for this detector family
should be read alongside a statement of label quality; comparisons across
papers with different annotation rules can otherwise mix detector performance
with labelling policy.

Second, pixel-level false-negative rates have an instance-level blind spot: the
pixel-wise segmentation formulation labels every trail pixel identically, so
merging two near-parallel trails into one mask can leave the pixel metrics used
here unchanged. This matters for newly launched satellites that appear as
several close, near-parallel trails: such masks can protect the image while
still giving unreliable trail counts.

Third, the boundary-placement finding suggests a practical reporting standard.
A majority of false-positive pixels lie within about one pixel of the labelled
boundary, and no tested change resolves that gap. The re-annotation
(Section~\ref{sec:ext_reannotation}) supports the interpretation that
part of the score is limited by annotation granularity. Evaluations of
thin-artefact detectors should therefore record a mask-width convention and
report a $\pm1$\,px boundary-tolerant operating point alongside the pixel
score, so that boundary convention and detection failure can be interpreted
separately.

For the motivating problem of satellite trails contaminating survey images, the
work supports high-recall trail masking and identifies which
errors matter for downstream use. Applying a one-pixel dilation before image
stacking is a plausible downstream masking test because it targets the measured
boundary error; its effect on stacking, photometry, and false masking remains
untested here. The classifier gate is best treated as a compute feature: it
skips $24.7\,\%$ of sampled-test U-Net forwards, and likely more under the
full-image patch distribution, while its precision gain is limited by the
$17.5\,\%$ inter-patch false-positive share. Because operating points depend on
the image distribution, an operational detector should re-select its threshold
on deployment-like data.

\chapter{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{ch:conclusions}

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This dissertation replicated the \citet{stoppa2024} U-Net plus probabilistic
Hough satellite-trail detector on a 178-image MeerLICHT subset. The architecture
was reconstructed from the released Keras model, and both the model and
threshold were fixed using validation data alone. The most stable comparisons
with the paper are recall and Hough completeness: five-seed sampled-test recall
is $0.923\pm0.007$ against $0.94$, and trail-pixel recall rises from $0.918$ to
$0.990$ on the full-coverage parity canvas after Hough. Pixel precision remains
below the published level ($0.847$ sampled, $0.780$ at parity) and depends on
the test-set class balance, leaving recall as the like-for-like comparison
across test populations.

The follow-up analyses make the precision gap interpretable: a one-pixel
boundary tolerance raises five-seed precision from $0.847$ to $0.946$, and
$57\,\%$ of false positives lie within one pixel of a labelled trail. A
single-author re-annotation probes the main ambiguity. The original labels, the
re-annotation, and the model all have median width $6$\,px, with no systematic
width bias. Under this design, model--re-annotation agreement is not
statistically distinguishable from original-label--re-annotation agreement. The
tested interventions have small or null effects in the tested settings:
classifier gate, attention U-Net, data efficiency, hard-negative mining, soft
labels, ensembling, and probability-stratified Hough. These results make simple
capacity or experimental-design explanations less likely without proving the
boundary interpretation. None closes the gap materially, so boundary-scale
annotation disagreement is the best-supported, but not proven, reading.

The broader methodological lesson is that faithful replication can diagnose
discrepancies as well as confirm headline results. Here, validation-only
selection and separate sampled/parity evaluations made the precision shortfall
interpretable. For astronomy, the useful outcome is a controlled case showing
that high-recall satellite-trail masking can coexist with boundary-limited pixel
precision. Evaluations of thin artefact detectors should therefore report pixel
scores with boundary-tolerant scores and mask-width conventions.

\section{Future work}
\label{sec:future}

Future work should separate three questions: how much annotators agree on trail
width, whether the detector transfers across instruments, and how masks affect
downstream image products. The strongest first step is a \emph{multi-annotator
audit}. Independent annotators would establish inter-annotator agreement and
turn the single-author audit of Section~\ref{sec:ext_reannotation} into a
stronger estimate of how much mask granularity contributes to the precision gap.
A smaller mask-cleaning experiment would test component-level choices, such as
removing isolated small objects before training and evaluation. The cleaned masks
would define a new target and leave the reported results unchanged.

A second priority is a \emph{labelled cross-instrument evaluation}, since the
DECam application here is qualitative only
(Figure~\ref{fig:decam_cold_montage}). This evaluation should include a
classical baseline following \citet{serrano2026}. Attention
\citep{oktay2018} and richer architectures could then be re-examined, since the
MeerLICHT matched-capacity null result may not generalise across instruments.

Further work should also address the limits of pixel-level scoring. An
object-level trail-counting metric would expose the merged near-parallel-trail
failure mode that pixel rates cannot detect. This could be made quantitative
with a synthetic test set containing close, parallel satellite trails. Trail
\emph{removal} is one natural downstream step: the detected mask and fitted
Hough geometry could be combined to interpolate across the trail. On 8-bit
renders, however, this would test visual interpolation rather than photometric
recovery. Together, these extensions would move the work from controlled
replication and error diagnosis toward practical satellite-trail mitigation.
